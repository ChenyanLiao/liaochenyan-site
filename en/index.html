01Peer-reviewed article2026
Peer-reviewed articleCommunications Biology · 2026

lncRNAs contribute to caste differentiation as a regulatory layer in ants

Ding, G., Lin, F., Zheng, J. et al. Communications Biology (2026)

What this work asks

Queens and workers can share essentially the same genome yet develop different bodies and perform very different work. Across developmental transcriptomes from the pharaoh ant and a leaf-cutting ant, this study identifies lncRNAs whose caste bias becomes progressively canalized, then connects those patterns with tissue-specific protein-coding networks, RNA-interference experiments, and juvenile-hormone responses.

My contribution

I contributed several parts of the bioinformatics analysis. To keep that collaborative role in proportion, the wording here follows the paper's formal author statement, which lists C.L. under Analysis.

CHARMM-GUI System Builder

An AI Agent skill for checking CHARMM-GUI membrane-system inputs, recovering interrupted jobs, identifying downloaded packages, and verifying GROMACS files.

Problem
A completed browser page, a completed download, and a checked GROMACS topology are three different states; none can prove the other two.
Liao's contribution
Turned input checks, browser-job capture, long-job recovery, archive identification, and GROMACS file inspection into steps an agent can execute one by one.
Public evidence
GitHub v2.2.0 release, AGPL-3.0 source, and public documentation; the public repository contains 130 tests and publishes structural-environment, pose-preservation, restraint-handoff, and Receipt 2.0 closure gates.
Boundary
An unofficial community project that does not replace CHARMM-GUI, run production simulation, or approve a system for production MD automatically.
Current direction
Continue strengthening structural-environment, ligand-pose, and restraint-handoff checks while keeping system building, MD authorization, and scientific judgment separate.

An unofficial community project. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by CHARMM-GUI, the Im Lab, or Lehigh University.

02Research Perspective

What computation can remove—and what it cannot prove

“Living matter evades the decay to equilibrium.”

— Erwin Schrödinger, What Is Life?

A computer can rank thousands of candidate molecules quickly; a laboratory cannot test them one by one. Much of my current work begins in that gap.

Generative models can propose molecules, virtual screening and docking can remove some poor candidates, and molecular dynamics can place a static pose into time. They answer different questions and share no universal authority: an attractive binding pose is not activity data, and a stable trajectory is not a mechanism.

I care about how a computational result reaches the bench: whether the input structure is trustworthy, why a parameter was chosen, where a run failed, and whether the surviving candidates can actually be made, measured, and checked again. Computation is less a judge than a sieve. The mesh determines what remains—and what might slip through.

01

A binding pose is not a mechanism

What I ask

How do observations at one biological scale change the questions we ask at another?

Why the distinction matters

Connecting scales matters because an elegant molecular explanation can still be incomplete without cellular, organismal, or environmental context.

02

Shorten the candidate list first

What I ask

How can AI reduce a large space of possibilities without turning prediction into an unsupported conclusion?

Why the distinction matters

The value of a model lies in the quality of the next question it enables, the evidence it helps prioritize, and the limits it makes explicit.

03

Make a calculation reproducible

What I ask

What must be recorded so another person can reproduce a computational result?

Why the distinction matters

Good tools should strengthen careful reasoning rather than hide assumptions behind speed, automation, or apparent certainty.

03Research Arc

From communities, insects, and genomes to molecules

“In nature, nothing exists alone.”

— Rachel Carson, Silent Spring
  1. 01Current direction

    AI for Science, AIDD & molecular design

    I currently use molecular generation, virtual screening, docking, and molecular dynamics to study how proteins and small molecules might meet—and which candidates should reach experiments first.

    These methods are good at forming a queue, not delivering a verdict. Scores, poses, and trajectories can support prioritization; they cannot be rewritten as activity or mechanism.

  2. 02Computational turn

    AI for Biology & computational methods

    This was the move from omics analysis toward structure- and molecule-centered computation: using existing models and software to prepare protein structures, compare candidate molecules, and examine simulation results.

    The transition changed both the scale of the questions and the knowledge they demand. I have had to add structural biology, chemistry, and computational chemistry to a background formed in organismal biology and genomics.

  3. 03Earlier foundation

    Evolutionary genomics & multi-omics

    Across transcriptomic, epigenetic, and evolutionary-genomic data, I learned to compare species, populations, and developmental stages—and to ask how those differences relate to phenotype and history.

    High-dimensional data readily produce significant results—and readily turn batch, sample composition, or annotation differences into apparent biology. That work taught me to inspect how the data were made before explaining why a plot separates.

  4. 04Organisms and behavior

    Insects, behavior & adaptation

    In insect and behavioral work, I watched individuals feed, choose, and interact—and how the same behavior changed with environment and developmental stage.

    Behavioral data returned an abstract difference to a particular moment: the same number can arise from very different actions and circumstances. See the animal first; decide how to turn it into data second.

  5. 05Widest frame

    Ecology, biodiversity & living systems

    My earliest work dealt with field plots, species composition, and environmental change. An organism never appeared alone; it lived among a community, a climate, and limited resources.

    Today I may focus on a pocket, a hydrogen bond, or a trajectory. I still remember that any molecular explanation must eventually return to a cell and an organism for testing.

04About

I Am What I Am

“You can't connect the dots looking forward; you can only connect them looking backwards.”

— Steve Jobs

I moved from fieldwork, insects, and genomes to proteins, small molecules, and code. The objects have become smaller and the screen busier; I still begin by asking what a difference means in a living system.

01

My current work centers on AI for Biology, AIDD, molecular design, and computational chemistry. A day may begin with a protein structure, pass through molecular generation, virtual screening, docking, or dynamics, and end with a small set of candidates awaiting experiments. The difficult part is often not another score, but knowing which assumptions produced it.

02

The set of synthesizable small molecules cannot be enumerated, while experimental time, material, and money are finite. AIDD does not create answers from nowhere. It removes some clearly poor choices and compares what remains. That can spare experiments a few detours; it does not turn a high computational score into activity in a tube.

03

Earlier I worked around ecology, insects, behavior, evolutionary genomics, transcriptomics, epigenetics, and heterosis. Field observation taught me to see how an organism lives; omics taught me how sampling and batch can shape a plot. Facing a molecular model now, I still ask the same plain question: how far is it from the biological phenomenon itself?

04

I also make two independent apps. GrindDialing grew from repeatedly recalibrating a grinder for a new bag of coffee; FilmDevMonitor from trying to time, read instructions, and keep track of steps in the darkroom at once. Photography lets me leave the parameter table for a while and wait for light, an insect, or a night sky to arrive in the right place.

Why a lobster?

I simply like this slightly absurd, forward-moving image. Research and software can be rigorous without requiring the person doing them to look solemn all the time. For me, the lobster is a playful reminder that however capable a tool becomes, people still decide where it should go.
